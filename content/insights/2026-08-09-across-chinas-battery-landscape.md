<div class="post-date">Aug 2026</div>

During a recent visit to China with Professor Jürgen Janek, I had an unusually concentrated opportunity to observe the country’s battery ecosystem from both academic and industrial perspectives. Our journey began in Beijing and continued through Wuhan, Hefei, Nanjing, Liyang, Ningbo, Ningde and Xiamen before concluding in Shanghai.

Along the way, we visited leading academic institutions and industrial organizations, including the Institute of Physics of the Chinese Academy of Sciences, the University of Science and Technology of China, Southeast University, Xiamen University, CATL, Gotion High-Tech and research institutes in the Yangtze River Delta region. I also had the opportunity to exchange ideas with prominent battery researchers including Professors Li Hong, Yang Yong and Zhang Xinping.

![Members of the visiting group in Beijing](/assets/posts/china-battery-visit/beijing-visit.jpg)

*Members of the visiting group in Beijing.*

This was the first time I had been able to observe China’s battery research and industry so intensively within such a short period. The experience broadened my perspective on solid-state batteries and left me with five reflections that I expect to revisit as my own work in the field develops.

## 1. Industry is not only translating science—it is generating scientific questions

What surprised me most was that, in certain areas of battery technology, industrial R&D appeared to be ahead of academic research in process integration, scale-up and the identification of failure modes. This differs from the semiconductor and functional-materials environments with which I am more familiar, where foundational discoveries and new device concepts often emerge first in research institutions.

Battery development is intensely application-driven. Many of its most important problems do not become fully visible in a small laboratory cell. They emerge during manufacturing, system integration and long-term operation. Industry is therefore not simply receiving knowledge from academia; production itself exposes new phenomena and generates scientific questions.

## 2. Intense competition also reveals a need for deeper breakthroughs

A second strong impression was the intensity of competition in solid-state battery research. It exists between companies and institutions, between technological routes and, at times, between approaches within the same organization.

In my view, this partly reflects the absence of a single theoretical or technological breakthrough that has decisively reorganized the field. Many teams are still working within related material systems and engineering frameworks, improving ionic conductivity, interfacial stability, cycle life, energy density, safety and manufacturability. These advances matter, but the resulting competition can become concentrated around incremental gains in similar performance metrics.

Deeper trust between companies and research institutions could help translate industrial failure modes into well-posed fundamental questions. In return, stronger physical understanding could open genuinely different material systems and technical pathways. Distinctive routes would allow companies to compete through differentiated ideas rather than only through marginal improvements along the same trajectory.

## 3. The next battery market is global

The scale and visibility of China’s battery industry were striking. Electric vehicles are already much more prominent in everyday life in China than in Germany, while markets such as Germany still have substantial room for growth. The [IEA’s Global EV Outlook 2025](https://www.iea.org/reports/global-ev-outlook-2025) similarly shows how unevenly electric mobility is developing across regions.

For Chinese battery companies with strong technology and manufacturing capabilities, the next phase will therefore be shaped not only by domestic competition but also by international expansion. Local supply chains, overseas engineering capacity, technical service and long-term partnerships will be essential for converting technological strength into durable global presence.

## 4. Established schools of thought bring both depth and risk

Across the institutions we visited, I noticed distinct and increasingly stable schools of thought around particular materials, cell concepts and research methods. Such continuity can be a major advantage: it preserves knowledge, supports long-term experimental capability and enables technical depth that cannot be built through short projects.

At the same time, mature networks can become inward-looking. If academic lineages, technical routes and collaboration patterns grow too fixed, unconventional ideas may find it harder to enter the discussion. The long-term question is how to retain accumulated expertise while encouraging cross-disciplinary exchange and allowing alternative approaches to be tested fairly.

## 5. Solid-state batteries remain a fundamental physics problem

The point that interests me most is how many basic scientific questions remain unresolved. Even discussions with leading researchers repeatedly returned to microscopic mechanisms that cannot yet be described within a unified physical picture.

A solid-state battery is not simply a materials-optimization problem. It is a multiscale, strongly coupled system involving ionic and electronic transport, interfacial reactions, defects, mechanical stress, chemical potentials, space-charge effects, phase transformations and non-equilibrium processes. In a broader physical sense, it is a problem with many interacting degrees of freedom whose collective behaviour cannot be understood by optimizing each component in isolation.

This is why I hope more researchers with backgrounds in physics will enter the field and work closely with chemists, materials scientists and engineers. Improving individual performance metrics remains essential, but deeper mechanistic understanding may be what ultimately enables the next major technological shift. The challenges and development targets are discussed in detail by [Jürgen Janek and Wolfgang G. Zeier in *Nature Energy*](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41560-023-01208-9).

## Looking ahead

This journey showed me not only the speed and scale of China’s battery development, but also a distinctive innovation ecosystem in which industrial demand, fundamental science and engineering are tightly intertwined.

I returned with a great deal of respect for the people working across this landscape—and with more questions than before. For me, that is one of the most valuable outcomes of a research visit: not a list of simple conclusions, but a clearer sense of which questions deserve sustained attention.
